The Land Between

This section describes the seas, mountains, rivers and faults of Türkiye and explains where the names Anatolia, Asia Minor, Turkey and Türkiye come from.

Modern Türkiye stands on two continents. Asian Türkiye, the great peninsula called Anatolia or Asia Minor, makes up about 97 per cent of the country. The other 3 per cent, Eastern Thrace, lies in Europe, yet it is home to about 12 per cent of the population. The two parts are divided by a chain of water about 361 kilometres long. The Bosphorus leads from the Black Sea into the small Sea of Marmara, and the Dardanelles lead on into the Aegean. Every ship sailing between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean has to pass through all three.

Inland the ground rises quickly. Between two mountain systems, the Pontic ranges along the Black Sea and the Taurus in the south, lies the high interior usually called the Anatolian plateau. Britannica points out that its relief is far more varied than the word plateau suggests. The higher ranges of the Taurus close it off from the Mediterranean coast. In the far east the land climbs to the volcanic massif of Mount Ararat, which reaches 5,137 metres close to the borders with Armenia and Iran and is the highest point in the country.

These eastern highlands are also where two great rivers of the ancient world begin. The Murat, the main headstream of the Euphrates, rises north of Lake Van near Mount Ararat and flows westward for 722 kilometres before it joins the Karasu to form the upper Euphrates. The Tigris rises in Lake Hazar, a small mountain lake south-east of Elazığ, and flows past the basalt walls of Diyarbakır before it forms the border with Syria. Both rivers then carry Anatolian water south to the plains of Mesopotamia.

The forces that raised these mountains also make the ground move. Anatolia rides on a small plate that is being forced westward as the Arabian plate pushes north against the Eurasian plate. Two great faults take up the movement, the North Anatolian Fault across the north of the country and the East Anatolian Fault in the south-east. From 1939 the North Anatolian Fault broke in a series of large earthquakes that moved from east to west over 60 years. The series began at Erzincan, where nearly 33,000 people died, and reached İzmit on 17 August 1999, where more than 17,000 people were killed.

Earthquakes run all through the written record. In Istanbul the earthquake of 1509 was so destructive that people called it kıyamet-i suğra, the ‘little apocalypse’, a name drawn from Islamic teaching that linked earthquakes with the end of the world. In February 2023 two earthquakes on the East Anatolian Fault, of magnitude 7.8 and 7.5, struck the south-east within hours of each other. More than 50,000 people died in Türkiye and over 7,000 in Syria, making it the deadliest earthquake in modern Turkish history.

The names of the land carry its history. Anatolia comes from the Greek anatolē, the east, originally the sunrise. Asia Minor, Latin for ‘lesser Asia’, is the other name long used for the peninsula. Turkey, the land of the Turks, came into English through Old French from the medieval Latin Turchia, which appears in western European texts by the 12th century. The Turks themselves say Türkiye. In 2022 the government asked the United Nations and other international organisations to use that form in English, and they agreed, although Turkey remains the everyday English name.

Few regions have been home to so many peoples and states. The earliest recorded inhabitants, the Hattians and Hurrians, lived here as early as about 2300 BCE. Hittites, Phrygians and Lydians followed. Greek settlers reached the west coast between about 1200 and 1000 BCE, and the Persians conquered Lydia in 546 BCE, as a survey of the history of Anatolia sets out. Alexander and his successors, Rome and Byzantium came after them, and then the Seljuk and Ottoman Turks.

The chapters that follow travel through this land roughly in order of time. They begin in the south-east with the stone monuments raised by hunter-gatherers at Göbekli Tepe and the houses of the first farmers, pass through the Bronze Age kingdoms and the Greek, Roman and Byzantine centuries, and continue with the Seljuks, the Ottomans and the Republic. A final chapter guides the traveller to the sites.

The First Monuments

How hunter-gatherers in the hills around Şanlıurfa raised carved stone pillars at Göbekli Tepe, Karahan Tepe and their neighbours, long before writing or cities.

Between about 9600 and 8200 BCE, groups of hunter-gatherers in the Germuş mountains of south-eastern Anatolia built a series of monumental round-oval and rectangular structures on the hill now called Göbekli Tepe. They lived in the Pre-Pottery Neolithic, the period before people in the region made pottery. The buildings are ringed by T-shaped limestone pillars up to 5.5 metres tall, many of them carved with wild animals. UNESCO, which added the site to its World Heritage List in 2018, describes the complex as probably linked to rituals, possibly funerary ones.

The German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt recognised the Neolithic pillars in 1994 and began excavating the following year with the Şanlıurfa Museum and the German Archaeological Institute in Istanbul. A Smithsonian magazine feature called the site ‘the world’s first temple’. Schmidt himself saw the great pillared buildings as belonging to the sphere of the dead, as Oliver Dietrich and colleagues explain in their account of his ideas. Later excavations have complicated that picture.

The pillars are often richly decorated. Some have arms, belts and loincloths carved in relief, which gives them human features. Foxes, boars, snakes, scorpions and vultures appear again and again, and Pillar 43 is known as the ‘Vulture Stone’. What the images meant is debated. One comparative study of ritual at Göbekli Tepe, Nevalı Çori, Çayönü and sites in the Levant argued that ritual there followed four basic principles. These were communality, dominant symbols, ideas of vitality such as fertility and life force, and close links between humans and animals.

Excavation continued after Schmidt. The Şanlıurfa Museum took over direction of the dig, with Lee Clare as field director for the German Archaeological Institute, and work between 2015 and 2019 found domestic buildings, rainwater channels, cisterns and evidence of cereal processing. These finds challenge the idea that the hill held temples and nothing else. The site had two main kinds of building. The monumental round to oval ones measure about 20 metres across. Smaller rectangular buildings, some with mostly plain pillars up to 2 metres high, were built around them, partly at the same time and partly later.

Laura Dietrich and colleagues analysed more than 7,000 grinding slabs, handstones, pestles and mortars from the site. Most had been used to process cereals, and microscopic plant remains confirm that cereals were present in large quantities. Animal bones point to large-scale hunting of gazelle between midsummer and autumn. No large storage facilities have been found, so the team argues that food was prepared for immediate use. Building and maintaining the monuments would have needed a considerable workforce, and the authors interpret the seasonal peaks of activity as evidence of large work feasts.

Göbekli Tepe is one of many sites of its kind. Surveys and excavations in Şanlıurfa province show that Pre-Pottery Neolithic centres cluster in the region, among them Karahan Tepe, Sayburç, Nevalı Çori, Ayanlar Höyük and Harbetsuvan Hill. Harbetsuvan, discovered in 2014 in the Tek Tek mountains some 53 kilometres east of Şanlıurfa, has produced T-shaped pillars, flint tools, grinding stones and stone vessels. Archaeologists call this group of sites the Taş Tepeler, the ‘stone hills’, and Göbekli Tepe lies near its centre.

At Karahan Tepe, another hill in the Urfa area, illegal digging exposed a T-shaped pillar carved with a snake. Bahattin Çelik, who described the site as a new cultural centre in 2011, compared the relief with similar ones from Göbekli Tepe. Karahan Tepe also has a megalithic structure of eleven pillars around a pool. One researcher, M. Sweatman, reads it as a lunisolar calendar, one of many interpretations of these sites. Birds appear here too. Garfinkel and colleagues note that bird imagery was unusually common at Karahan Tepe and other sites of about 9800–8700 BCE, and link it to the move from a mobile way of life to a settled one.

Nevalı Çori, another Pre-Pottery Neolithic site in the region, had a so-called cult building with pillars like those at Göbekli Tepe, and almost all of its limestone sculptures were found inside that building. Its 665 clay figurines, nine in ten of them showing men and women, came instead from the houses. Schmidt used this pattern to argue that clay served the rituals of the living and stone the dead, although Dietrich and colleagues note that his reading is open to debate. The next chapter turns to central Anatolia, where people were beginning to farm and herd.

The First Farmers

How people in central Anatolia settled in villages, herded sheep and goats, traded obsidian and built Çatalhöyük, and how Anatolian farmers carried farming into Europe.

While hunter-gatherers were raising pillars in the south-east, people on the central Anatolian plateau were settling down. Aşıklı Höyük is the earliest known mound settlement in central Anatolia from the Neolithic period before pottery. Its oldest levels, made up of round houses, date from about 9000 to 8200 BCE, and the site preserves an unusually detailed record of the first thousand years of Neolithic life in the region. Settled farming communities first emerged in parts of the Fertile Crescent in the tenth and early ninth millennia BCE, and according to one genetic study they had appeared in central Anatolia by 8300 BCE.

The animal bones from Aşıklı record one of the earliest steps towards herding. At first people ate a broad range of wild hoofed animals and small game. Within a few centuries their meat came mainly from sheep, and by about 8200 BCE animals were being kept inside the village, where layers of dung built up between the houses. A later study dates the start of this local process to about 8400 BCE and calls its first stage ‘catch-and-grow’. Each season villagers captured wild lambs and kids in the surrounding highlands and raised them for several months before slaughter. Over time this grew into a large herding economy, which makes Aşıklı an independent centre of sheep and goat domestication.

Central Anatolia also had a resource that people valued far away. Obsidian, a volcanic glass, was used across the Near East to make tools and luxury objects. Geochemical analysis shows that the prehistoric sources known to have been worked include Göllü Dağ and Nenezi Dağ in Cappadocia, and Bingöl, Nemrut Dağ and a source near Lake Van in the east. Cappadocian obsidian was widely used in central Anatolia. It travelled west as far as the Sea of Marmara and east to the middle Euphrates, and then south along the Mediterranean coast to the Negev. Obsidian from the Lake Van area went down the Tigris and Euphrates to the shores of the Persian Gulf.

Çatalhöyük lies in central Anatolia, about 450 kilometres from Göbekli Tepe. Its eastern mound holds eighteen levels of Neolithic occupation dated between 7400 and 6200 BCE. The town had no streets. Houses were built back to back without doors or windows, and people climbed in and out through openings in the roof. According to National Geographic, the rooftops served as the town’s streets and plazas. Estimates of the population have fallen. Older claims ranged from 3,500 to 10,000 people, whereas a 2024 study suggests 600 to 800 in an average year of the Middle phase, about 6700–6500 BCE.

The people of Çatalhöyük grew domesticated cereals and pulses and kept domestic sheep and goats, yet they also relied on wild cattle, boar, deer and wild equids. At Göbekli Tepe, by contrast, all the plant and animal foods identified were wild species, as Hodder and colleagues point out. The two places also used art in different ways. At Göbekli Tepe the imagery was concentrated in separate buildings, often described as temples. At Çatalhöyük the wall paintings, reliefs and sculptures were found inside the houses themselves.

Studies of the skeletons suggest that life became harder over time. Between about 7100 and 5950 BCE the people of Çatalhöyük show rising levels of disease and workload, greater mobility and some violence between individuals. Another study found broadly similar access to staple foods and no evidence of lasting, institutionalised inequality. Settlement on the East Mound came to an end soon after 6000 BCE, according to a Bayesian analysis of 56 radiocarbon dates.

Farming soon spread beyond central Anatolia. It reached western Anatolia by the early seventh millennium BCE and moved into Europe at almost the same time. Genomes from nine Neolithic individuals from central Anatolia show that the earliest farmers there belonged to the same gene pool as the first Neolithic migrants into Europe. Their genetic diversity was strikingly low at first, similar to that of European hunter-gatherer groups, and it rose with the Pottery Neolithic to levels later found among early European farmers.

Ancient DNA studies have established that Europe’s Neolithic populations were descended from Anatolian migrants, who mixed only to a limited extent with the hunter-gatherers already living there. The mix varied from place to place. A study of 180 ancient DNA samples from Neolithic and Chalcolithic Hungary, Germany and Spain found different sources and proportions of hunter-gatherer ancestry in each region and over time, and frequent mixing between groups. The first farmers of Europe therefore carried an Anatolian inheritance that local encounters then reshaped.

Merchants and Hittite Kings

How Anatolia entered written history through Assyrian merchants at Kanesh, and how the Hittites built an empire from Hattusa that rivalled Egypt.

Long before writing reached Anatolia, its rulers were buried with remarkable metalwork. The clearest evidence comes from Alacahöyük in Çorum province, one of the first excavations run by the Turkish Republic. Between 1935 and 1940 archaeologists opened fourteen graves there, now called the Royal Tombs and dated to about 2500 to 2000 BCE. The dead lay with gold and silver jewellery, weapons, metal vessels and bronze standards in the form of stylised sun discs, stags and bulls. The tombs are the main evidence for the Hattians, the people who lived here before the Hittites. They are roughly as old as the royal graves of Ur.

Writing arrived with foreign merchants. In the 20th century BCE traders from Assur, in what is now northern Iraq, settled beside the city of Kanesh, whose mound, Kültepe, lies about 20 kilometres north-east of Kayseri. Their quarter was a kārum, or trading post, the most important of several in Anatolia. The Assyrians bought tin from the Iranian plateau and textiles from Babylonia, carried them by donkey caravan on a journey of six to eight weeks, and sold them for Anatolian silver and gold, which went back to Assur.

The merchants wrote everything down. About 23,500 clay tablets have been found at Kültepe so far. They are the oldest written documents from Anatolia, written in the Old Assyrian dialect in a simplified cuneiform and dating mainly to the 19th and 18th centuries BCE. They come from private family archives and include letters, contracts, loans, caravan accounts, inheritance records and court decisions. Many letters were written by women in Assur, who produced the textiles for export while their husbands traded in Anatolia. UNESCO added the archive to its Memory of the World Register in 2015.

Local kings fought for the plateau. Anitta of Kussara took Kanesh, then destroyed Hattusa and cursed any king who resettled it. The Hittites later called their own language nesili, 'the language of Nesa', another name for Kanesh. About a century after Anitta, a king who probably took the name Hattusili, 'the one from Hattusa', ignored the curse and made the city his capital around 1650 BCE, founding what historians call the Old Kingdom. His successor Mursili I destroyed Aleppo and then marched some 2,000 kilometres to sack Babylon, traditionally dated to about 1595 BCE, although the chronology is debated. He did not stay, and the Kassites took Babylonia.

The Hittites became an imperial power in the 14th century BCE under Suppiluliuma I. He crossed the Euphrates, sacked Wassukkani, capital of the rival kingdom of Mitanni, and took control of northern Syria. In 1274 BCE Muwatalli II met Ramesses II at Kadesh on the Orontes, near today's border between Lebanon and Syria, in one of the largest chariot battles ever fought. The Hittites lured the Egyptians into an ambush with some 2,500 chariots. The battle ended without a clear result, and both sides claimed victory.

About fifteen years later Hattusili III and Ramesses II made peace. Their agreement, usually dated to about 1259 BCE (some sources give 1269 BCE), is the oldest peace treaty whose text survives. It promised lasting peace, non-aggression, mutual help and the return of fugitives. The Hittite copy, in Akkadian on clay tablets, was found at Hattusa in the excavations of 1906 to 1912, and fragments are now in Istanbul, Ankara, Berlin and St Petersburg. In 1970 Turkey gave the United Nations a copper replica by the sculptor Sadi Çalık, which hangs outside the Security Council chamber in New York.

Hattusa, beside the village of Boğazkale, was at its height between about 1375 and 1200 BCE. UNESCO, which listed it in 1986, highlights its temples, royal residences, the carved Lions' Gate and King's Gate, and more than 8 kilometres of walls. In 1906 Hugo Winckler and Theodore Makridi began digging on the citadel of Büyükkale and found the royal archive. The Boğazköy tablets now number nearly 25,000. About 2 kilometres away lies Yazılıkaya, an open-air sanctuary where two processions of gods converge on the storm god Teshub and the goddess Hebat. It was completed under Tudhaliya IV in the 13th century BCE.

Scholars could read the cuneiform script of the tablets but not their language until 1915, when the Czech scholar Bedřich Hrozný showed that Hittite is Indo-European. It is the oldest recorded language of that family, a distant relative of Latin, German and English. Its close cousin Luwian was spoken across southern and western Anatolia and written both in cuneiform and in a hieroglyphic script of its own. Much of the Hurrian religion shown at Yazılıkaya reached Hattusa through Kizzuwatna, a kingdom on the Cilician plain centred on the cult city of Kummanni, which the Hittites later annexed.

The end came in the decades around 1200 BCE. The last known king, Suppiluliuma II, came to the throne around 1207 BCE, and then the records fall silent. Excavations suggest that Hattusa was gradually abandoned by the court and officials over several decades, and that the fires which burned parts of the city came after they had left. The causes are still debated. Drought over several years, earthquakes, internal strife and outside pressure have all been proposed. Luwian hieroglyphic inscriptions carried on in smaller states of south-central Anatolia and Syria until about 700 BCE.

Troy and Its Nine Cities

The mound of Hisarlık holds nine cities built over 4,000 years, the story of their excavation and the long argument about Homer's Trojan War.

Troy lies on a low mound called Hisarlık in north-western Türkiye. People lived on it for about 4,000 years. Each time the town was ruined, usually by fire or earthquake, the new settlers levelled the debris and built on top of it. The result is a stack of cities that archaeologists number from Troy I at the bottom to Troy IX at the top. UNESCO added the site to the World Heritage List in 1998, calling its remains the most significant evidence of the first contact between the civilisations of Anatolia and the growing Mediterranean world.

The first walled village, Troy I, dates from about 3000 to 2550 BCE. A recent discovery by the Turkish team now digging at the site takes Troy's history back some 600 years. The great Late Bronze Age city is Troy VI, from about 1750 to 1300 BCE, followed by Troy VIIa, which lasted until about 1180 BCE. At the top is Troy IX, the Roman city of Ilion. The Romans sacked it in 85 BCE, but Augustus and his successors later gave it fine public buildings, because they traced their ancestry to the Trojan prince Aeneas.

The modern search began with Frank Calvert, a man of English descent who owned a farm nearby and part of the mound. He made trial excavations at Hisarlık in 1865, cleared part of the theatre and identified the site with classical Ilion. Calvert convinced Heinrich Schliemann that Homer's Troy lay beneath the classical ruins, and the two became partners. Schliemann began digging in April 1870, before he had an official permit from the Ottoman authorities. UNESCO regards his excavations as a starting point of modern archaeology. Schliemann, however, left Calvert's name out of his announcements and books, and Calvert, who died in 1908, was never officially credited with the discovery.

In May 1873 Schliemann announced his most famous find, a hoard of gold, jewellery and other valuables, including a golden diadem, which he called Priam's Treasure. He smuggled the hoard, around 8,000 objects, out of the Ottoman Empire. The name was wrong. The treasure came from Troy II, a burnt layer of the third millennium BCE, more than a thousand years older than any Priam of Homer's story. Most of it went to the Neues Museum in Berlin. During the Second World War it was hidden beneath Berlin Zoo, where Soviet soldiers found it. They took it to Moscow, and most of it is still in the Pushkin State Museum of Fine Arts.

Wilhelm Dörpfeld dug for two seasons, in 1893 and 1894. He exposed the walls of Troy VI, up to five metres thick with large towers, and numbered the layers from I to IX. He believed Troy VI was Homer's city, although later study pointed rather to Troy VIIa. Carl Blegen of the University of Cincinnati led seven seasons from 1932 to 1938, identified 47 strata and concluded that Troy VI had been destroyed by an earthquake. From 1988 to 2005 Manfred Korfmann led a team from Tübingen and Cincinnati and found a lower town of about 20 hectares south of the citadel. Since 2013 Rüstem Aslan of Çanakkale Onsekiz Mart University has directed the excavation.

Whether Homer's war took place is still argued. Troy VI may have been damaged by war around 1250 BCE, although there is also evidence of a major earthquake at that time. Troy VIIa was destroyed violently, probably between about 1230 and 1180 BCE, and arrowheads have been found in its burnt debris. Hittite texts add a political setting. They name a western Anatolian kingdom called Wilusa, whose king Alaksandu made a treaty with the Hittite king Muwatalli II around 1280 BCE. Most scholars accept Wilusa as the Hittite form of Ilios, a Greek name for Troy, and Taruisa as a form of Troia. The name Alaksandu recalls Alexandros, a Trojan prince of Greek legend.

The same texts show Wilusa caught up in disputes between the Hittites and Ahhiyawa, a western power usually identified with the Greeks. Yet they never say that Ahhiyawa attacked Wilusa, and the historian Trevor Bryce warns that they offer no evidence of an actual Trojan War. Archaeology can show a city destroyed by war, but not who the attackers were or why they came. On the mound today the walls of Troy VI and the remains of the later cities are still visible, and finds from the excavations are shown in the Troy Museum, which opened near the site, at Tevfikiye in Çanakkale province, on 10 October 2018.

Phrygia, Urartu and Lydia

After the Hittites fell, Neo-Hittite kingdoms, the Phrygia of King Midas, Urartu around Lake Van and Lydia, home of the first coins, rose across Anatolia.

After the Hittite Empire collapsed around 1200 BCE, a number of minor kingdoms grew up in south-eastern Anatolia and northern Syria. Their art followed Hittite models and their inscriptions often used Luwian hieroglyphs, so scholars call them Neo-Hittite or Syro-Hittite states. The Assyrians called the region the 'Lands of Hatti'. At Karkamiš, or Carchemish, the leading city, the dynasty of the great Hittite king Suppiluliuma I held on at least until the 11th century BCE. From the 10th century Aramaeans became the dominant people in most of these states, and at the end of the 8th century Assyria absorbed them into its empire.

On the central plateau the Phrygians built a new state. Its capital, Gordion, is the mound of Yassıhöyük where the Sakarya and Porsuk rivers meet. Radiocarbon dates show that the settlement slipped free of Hittite control in the 12th century BCE and became the Phrygian capital early in the 9th. Until the 7th century Phrygia dominated central Anatolia. About a hundred burial mounds rise around the citadel, and most of the 43 excavated date from between 850 and 530 BCE. One, the 8th-century Tumulus 52, held more than 3,000 amber beads from the Baltic. UNESCO added Gordion to the World Heritage List in 2023.

A great fire destroyed the citadel and ended what archaeologists call the Early Phrygian phase. For decades scholars dated it to about 690 BCE, one of the few seemingly secure dates of the Anatolian Iron Age, and linked it to the death of Midas in a Cimmerian attack. Tree rings and radiocarbon dates on short-lived samples have overturned that date and moved the fire to the late 9th century BCE, well before the time of Midas. One reviewer called the affair a cautionary tale about building elaborate interpretations on flimsy foundations.

Midas is the most famous king of Phrygia. Eusebius dated his reign from 738 to 696 BCE, in line with Assyrian records, and tradition held that it ended when invading Cimmerians destroyed his city. The largest tumulus, known as Tumulus MM or the Midas Mound, still stands about 53 metres high and was once 70 or 80 metres. In 1957 a team from the Pennsylvania University Museum entered its wooden burial chamber. A man aged 60 to 65 lay on a bed among more than 350 bronze vessels and ornaments and wooden furniture. The excavator, Rodney Young, doubted that this was Midas and preferred his predecessor Gordios. The new chronology dates the tomb to about 740 BCE.

Far to the east, Urartu dominated eastern Anatolia, the Caucasus and north-western Iran between the 9th and 6th centuries BCE. Its capital, Tushpa, was the citadel of Van, 1.5 kilometres east of Lake Van, and its greatest enemy was Assyria to the south. On the Van plain, 1,750 metres above sea level, many crops fail without irrigation. Urartian engineers built more than ten dams and reservoirs fed by the springs of Erek mountain, and most still work after more than 2,700 years. Inscriptions of King Minua, who reigned from 810 to 785 or 780 BCE, record fortresses, temples and irrigation canals around Malazgirt. One of his canals was called Mezaiani, the earlier form of the town's name.

In the west, Lydia reached its peak under its last two kings, Alyattes and Croesus, who ruled from Sardis. Alyattes took control of Phrygia, which Cimmerian raids had weakened. Coinage began in Lydia in the second half of the 7th century BCE. The first coins were made of electrum, a natural alloy of gold and silver, and Alyattes was the first king to issue them. His son Croesus, king from about 560 to 546 BCE, struck the first gold coins of standard purity and created the first system of gold and silver coins used side by side.

Along the coast stood the Ionian Greek cities, among them Miletus and Ephesus. Alyattes formed ties of guest-friendship, the Greek xenia, with the coastal cities. Croesus completed the conquest of the Greek cities of the coast and, according to one recent study, made them pay tribute and send soldiers to Sardis. Only Miletus kept the terms it had agreed with Alyattes. Yet Croesus also courted the Greeks. He paid towards the rebuilding of the temple of Artemis at Ephesus, the Artemisium, sent offerings to Delphi and other shrines, and was known for his friendliness to Greek visitors.

The end came from Persia. Croesus sought allies in Greece and Egypt, but the Persian king Cyrus moved first. Croesus lost a battle in Cappadocia and fell back on Sardis, which the Persians took. At its western fortification, excavators found croeseids, gold and silver coins with the foreparts of a lion and a bull, sealed under debris from Cyrus's capture of Sardis in the 540s BCE. Beside one lay the skeleton of a young man, probably killed in the battle. The usual date is 546 BCE, though some scholars prefer 547. Legend had Apollo save Croesus from a pyre. The Greek cities of the coast passed to Persia, and in time rose against it in the Ionian revolt.

Alexander and the Hellenistic Kingdoms

Alexander's march through Anatolia and the kingdoms that followed him, from Pergamon's library to the Galatian Celts, Pontus and the mountaintop gods of Commagene.

In the spring of 334 BCE Alexander of Macedon crossed the Hellespont, today's Dardanelles, to attack the Persian Empire of Darius III. His first battle in Asia came in May 334 BCE at the river Granicus, against a hastily gathered army under regional governors, the satraps. His senior general Parmenion advised waiting for the next day. Alexander crossed at once and took the enemy by surprise. The victory gave the Macedonians a foothold on enemy soil, refilled their empty supply stores and encouraged key Greek states to back the campaign.

In 333 BCE Alexander reached Gordium, the capital of Phrygia, south-west of today's Ankara. There he was shown the chariot of the city's legendary founder, its yoke lashed to the pole with an intricate knot whose ends were hidden. Tradition said that only the future conqueror of Asia would untie it. In the popular account Alexander sliced through it with his sword. Earlier versions have him cutting into the knot or drawing out the pole. A 'Gordian knot' still means a problem that only bold action can solve.

Later that year Alexander faced Darius III in person for the first time. The armies met near Issus, close to today's İskenderun, usually dated to 5 November 333 BCE. They fought on either side of a small river the ancient writers called the Pinarus, which several scholars identify with the Payas river. Here only about 2.6 kilometres separate the sea from the mountains, so the much larger Persian army could not use its numbers. Darius fled, first in his chariot and then on horseback. His mother, wife and children fell into Alexander's hands, and he treated them with dignity.

Alexander died in Babylon in June 323 BCE and left no clear heir. His generals, the Diadochi or 'successors', fought over the empire for more than twenty years. At Ipsus in 301 BCE Seleucus and Lysimachus defeated Antigonus the One-Eyed, and Seleucus took eastern Anatolia and northern Syria. Seleucid power in Anatolia lasted until Rome and Pergamon defeated Antiochus III at Magnesia in 190 BCE. By the Treaty of Apamea in 188 BCE the Seleucids gave up all of Asia west of the Taurus Mountains, and large areas went to Rome's allies, Pergamon and Rhodes.

The main winner in the west was Pergamon, a fortified hill above the Bakırçay plain whose lower town lies beneath today's Bergama. Its Attalid kings made it a centre of learning. Eumenes II built a library said to hold some 200,000 volumes, second only to Alexandria. According to an old story, Ptolemy of Egypt grew jealous and banned the export of papyrus, so Pergamon turned to prepared animal skins. Writing on leather was older than that, yet the city became the leading supplier, and the English word parchment comes from the Latin pergamenum.

Eumenes II also built the Great Altar. Its frieze, about 113 metres long, shows the Olympian gods fighting the giants, a myth widely read as an image of Pergamon's victories over the Galatians. Carl Humann began official excavations on the acropolis in 1878 and shipped the frieze slabs to Berlin. The rebuilt altar is the centrepiece of the Pergamonmuseum, closed for renovation until 2027. In 2014 UNESCO inscribed Pergamon and its Multi-Layered Cultural Landscape, with its temples, theatres, gymnasium and library set into the slopes of the hill.

The Galatians were Celtic Gauls. King Nicomedes I of Bithynia invited them into Anatolia in 278 BCE as allies in his wars, and they settled in the centre of the peninsula, which took the name Galatia from them. The Tolistobogii held the west near Pessinus, the Tectosages the middle around Ancyra, today's Ankara, and the Trocmi the east around Tavium. A council of 300 met at a sacred oak grove called Drynemeton. Attalus I of Pergamon defeated them between about 241 and 237 BCE and again in 232 BCE, and took the title 'Saviour'.

On the Black Sea side a Persian satrap, Mithridates I Ktistes, founded the kingdom of Pontus in 301 BCE with its capital at Amaseia, today's Amasya. The rock tombs of its early kings are cut into the cliffs of Mount Harşena above the town. The last great king, Mithridates VI Eupator, ruled from 120 to 63 BCE and became the enemy Rome feared most since Hannibal. In 89 or 88 BCE he organised the massacre of Romans and Italians across Asia Minor; ancient writers put the dead at 80,000 or even 150,000. Pompey defeated him in 66 BCE, and he died in 63 BCE.

Far to the south-east, between the Taurus Mountains and the Euphrates, lay the small kingdom of Commagene. Its king Antiochus I, who reigned from 69 to 34 BCE, traced his line through both Greek and Persian legends. On the summit of Mount Nemrut, 2,134 metres high near today's Adıyaman, he built a temple-tomb whose mound of stone chips is 50 metres high and 145 metres across. Colossal seated statues show Antiochus among gods that merge Greek and Iranian figures, such as Zeus-Oromasdes and Heracles-Artagnes. UNESCO inscribed Nemrut Dağ in 1987. Rome finally annexed Commagene in 72 CE.

Cilicia: Adana, Tarsus and Kastabala

The fertile plain around Adana, from the Bronze Age kingdom of Kizzuwatna to the fire-walking priestesses of Kastabala and a Roman bridge still standing over the Seyhan.

Cilicia is the old name for the south-eastern corner of Türkiye's Mediterranean coast. Its eastern half, called Cilicia Pedias in antiquity and Çukurova in Turkish today, is a fertile alluvial plain. The Taurus and Amanus mountains close it in on the west, north and east, and the sea lies to the south. Three rivers cross it, the Tarsus, the Seyhan and the Ceyhan, and their lower courses have shifted over the millennia. Since the Neolithic the plain has been an interface between Anatolia and the Levant. Archaeologists describe it as one of the most fertile regions of the ancient Near East, at the junction of Anatolia, Syria and Mesopotamia, and Cyprus.

The plain enters written history in the Late Bronze Age as the kingdom of Kizzuwatna, first attested at the end of the 16th century BCE. Its people spoke Luwian and Hurrian, and its town of Kummanni was a Hurrian religious centre. Hittite texts call the heartland of the kingdom Adaniya, the earliest known form of the name Adana. For a time Kizzuwatna kept its independence as a buffer between the Hittites and the kingdom of Mittani to the east. That ended when its king Sunassura made a treaty with the Hittite king Tudhaliya I, and the plain passed under Hittite rule.

After the fall of the Hittite empire the plain became a Neo-Hittite kingdom known as Hiyawa or Que. Its towns can be studied at Sirkeli Höyük in Adana province, one of the largest settlement mounds on the plain, where excavations from 2013 to 2019 traced a residential quarter of the lower town from the 11th to the 7th century BCE. Further north-east, in today's Kadirli district of Osmaniye, a lord named Azatiwada, who served the rulers of Que, founded a town called Azatiwataya. In the 8th century BCE he had an account of his deeds carved there in two languages, Phoenician and hieroglyphic Luwian.

The two versions of the Karatepe bilingual tell the same story. The Phoenician text was composed first and then translated into Luwian, and because Phoenician could already be read, the inscription became the key to deciphering Luwian hieroglyphs. Excavation began in 1946 under Helmuth Bossert and continued under Halet Çambel, and in 1958 the site opened as the Karatepe-Aslantaş Open-Air Museum. The inscriptions are on UNESCO's tentative list for World Heritage status, and in 2025 they joined the Memory of the World register, the UNESCO list of documentary heritage.

West of Adana lies Tarsus, on the river the Greeks called the Kydnos. The historian C. E. Bosworth, in a study of the city and the Arab-Byzantine frontier, explains its long prosperity by three advantages. It had the fertile soil of the plain and a sheltered harbour open to the eastern Mediterranean, and it commanded the southern end of the Cilician Gates, the pass through the Taurus Mountains into central Anatolia. Xenophon and Alexander the Great both led armies through that pass. Tarsus is not named in Hittite texts. It first appears firmly in Assyrian records, where a clay tablet says that Sennacherib, king from 705 to 681 BCE, rebuilt the city after a revolt in Que.

Under the Persians, Bosworth writes, Tarsus was probably still governed by local princes, who struck coins with Aramaic inscriptions in the 4th century BCE. The oldest part of the city is the mound of Gözlükule, which stayed inhabited from the Bronze Age into Roman times. It was explored in the mid-19th century and excavated in the mid-20th, and Boğaziçi University reopened excavations there early in this century. Because modern Tarsus is built on top of the ancient city, ancient remains turn up at many points in the modern town. The Roman road excavated in the heart of Tarsus lay under about four metres of sediment left by the Kydnos.

About 12 kilometres north-north-west of Osmaniye, near the Ceyhan, the ancient Pyramos, lie the ruins of Hierapolis-Kastabala. The city was the cult centre of Artemis Perasia, the Greek name for the old Anatolian goddess Kubaba, and the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes renamed it Hieropolis after her. Its sanctuary was famous for a fire-walking rite. The Greek geographer Strabo wrote in his Geography that “at Castabala is the temple of Artemis Perasia, where it is said the priestesses walk with naked feet unhurt over hot embers.” The earliest mention of the city dates from 333 BCE, when Alexander the Great visited it.

In the 1st century BCE Kastabala was the capital of Tarkondimotos, a local ruler and the son of Strato. Pompey confirmed him as ruler in or after 66 BCE, and Mark Antony recognised him as king around 38 BCE, with the title Philantonios. Tarkondimotos died fighting for Antony at Actium in 31 BCE. His dynasty kept its small kingdom until 17 CE, when the emperor Tiberius added it to the Roman provinces. In the 2nd and 3rd centuries CE the city gained a colonnaded street and monumental buildings. After the city was abandoned, its castle on the old acropolis was used by Armenians and then by Crusaders, who called it Boudoin, a name that became today's Bodrum Kalesi.

West of Kastabala stands the other great ancient city of the plain, Anazarbos, known in Turkish as Anavarza, which is on UNESCO's tentative list for World Heritage status. A planning study published in 2026 calls Anavarza one of the largest archaeological complexes of Cilicia, a monumental urban site in a landscape historically associated with the ancient physician Pedanius Dioscorides. Official figures cited in that study show visitor numbers rising substantially between 2022 and 2024. Its authors propose a heritage route with Adana as the gateway city, Kozan as the staging town and Anavarza as the main site, so that visitors are spread out before crowds begin to damage the landscape.

Adana's own great monument is the Stone Bridge, Taşköprü, over the Seyhan. It is usually dated to the reign of Hadrian, probably the first half of the 2nd century CE. An inscription now in the Adana Museum names an architect called Auxentius, perhaps the man of that name active around 384, and so some scholars date the bridge to the 4th century instead. It is 310 metres long and 11.4 metres wide. It was built with 21 or 26 arches, depending on the source, and 14 are visible today. The bridge carried the trade route from the Mediterranean to inner Anatolia and Persia.

The bridge stayed in daily use for most of its long life. Motor traffic crossed it until 2007, when it was closed to vehicles, and today it belongs to pedestrians and hosts public events. Age has not protected it from every harm, and the Hürriyet Daily News has reported repeated cases of vandals spraying graffiti on its stones. Together, the bridge, the inscriptions of Karatepe, the embers of Kastabala and the buried streets of Tarsus show one plain rebuilt again and again by new rulers, each drawn by the same fertile soil and the same routes between Anatolia and the Levant.

Roman Asia and the first Christians

How western Anatolia became the Roman province of Asia, and how Christianity spread there from Paul's journeys through persecution to the councils of Nicaea and Chalcedon.

Rome gained western Anatolia by inheritance. The kingdom of Pergamon was ruled by the Attalid kings from a fortified hill above today's Bergama, and when the last of them, Attalus III, died in 133 BCE, he bequeathed his kingdom to Rome. The Romans called their new province simply Asia. The name Asia Minor, now used for the whole peninsula, came much later. The Christian historian Orosius is the first writer known to have used it, around 400 CE, to set apart the region where Paul the Apostle had spread Christianity.

Sardis, the old capital of Lydia, which Rome had handed to the kings of Pergamon in 188 BCE, passed to Rome with the rest of the kingdom. It soon became the centre of one of the province's nine conventus, its judicial districts. The province was fought over for decades. In 89 or 88 BCE Mithridates VI of Pontus organised the massacre of Romans and Italians across Asia Minor, and the wars against him ended only when Pompey defeated him in 66 BCE. By the time of Augustus peace and confidence had returned.

Sardis shows how a city of the province could recover from disaster. In 17 CE an earthquake devastated the city. Reconstruction went on well into the 2nd century, and by then Sardis probably had around 100,000 inhabitants. The fertile Hermus plain and the city's position inland, on the road to the Roman east, both favoured it. Under the Severan emperors the city enjoyed great prosperity and completed much building work.

The clearest sign of that wealth is the Marble Court of the city's bath-gymnasium, first built with the gymnasium under the emperor Lucius Verus in 165 CE. In 212 and 213 the court was elaborately enlarged with pavilions and a monumental gate, and most of the head-capitals found at Sardis, column tops carved with human heads, come from its colonnade. Other Roman remains include columned streets and a synagogue of the city's Jewish community. In July 2025 Sardis and the Lydian burial mounds of Bin Tepe were inscribed as a World Heritage Site.

The Acts of the Apostles describes how Paul of Tarsus carried the Christian message across Anatolia. On his first journey, around 46 to 48 CE, he and Barnabas landed at Perga in Pamphylia and pressed inland to Antioch in Pisidia. There Paul preached in the synagogue and won Jewish and Gentile converts until opponents had the pair expelled. At Iconium, today's Konya, they learned of a plot to kill them and moved on to Lystra, where Paul was stoned, and then to Derbe. Paul returned to Iconium in 50 and 53. On his third journey he spent about two years preaching at Ephesus.

Paul saw Ephesus as the perfect base for his mission. The city stood on the estuary of the Kaystros, and UNESCO, which lists it as a World Heritage Site, calls it an outstanding example of a Roman port city. Its monuments include Hadrian's Temple, the Library of Celsus, dedicated in 110 CE, and the Great Theatre, the scene, according to Acts, of a riot by the city's silversmiths against Paul. Ephesus heads the list of seven churches to which the Book of Revelation is addressed. Revelation names them as Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia and Laodicea, all in today's Türkiye.

Roman officials were unsure how to treat the new faith. In about 111, while governing Bithynia, Pliny the Younger executed Christians who refused to worship the emperor and the Roman gods, then asked the emperor Trajan whether they should be punished for the name of Christian itself. Trajan replied that Christians properly brought before a governor should be punished, and that governors should not hunt for them. Diocletian later took a far harsher line. After incidents at Nicomedia, where a church stood across from his palace, edicts of 303 and 304 ordered churches destroyed, sacred books seized, clergy imprisoned and death for anyone who refused to sacrifice to the gods. In 311 the emperor Galerius admitted that the persecution had failed and proclaimed an edict of tolerance.

Full legal rights followed in 313, when Constantine and his fellow emperor Licinius agreed the Edict of Milan. It allowed Christians to organise churches and ordered confiscated property returned. Constantine hoped a general council would settle a dispute in the eastern church over the teaching of Arius of Alexandria, who held that Christ was a created being, more than human but less than God. In 325 the emperor called the first ecumenical council of the church to Nicaea, today's İznik. The council condemned Arius and adopted the Creed of Nicaea, which declared Christ "begotten, not made" and homoousios, "of one substance", with the Father.

The fourth ecumenical council met at Chalcedon in 451. About 520 bishops or their representatives attended, making it the largest and best documented of the early councils. It declared Christ "perfect in deity and perfect in humanity", in two natures, without being mixed, divided or separated, and rejected the teaching that Christ had only one nature. From then on the Oriental Orthodox churches, among them the Armenian Apostolic, Coptic and Syriac Orthodox churches, were largely out of contact with the other main branches of Christianity. The Roman and Greek churches called them monophysites. They call their own view miaphysite, a single nature in which divinity and humanity are equally present.

Byzantium, the New Rome

How the Roman Empire lived on at Constantinople for over a thousand years, building Hagia Sophia, surviving plague and sieges, losing its capital in 1204 and winning it back.

In 324 Constantine the Great defeated his last rival and reunited the Roman Empire. He chose the old Greek city of Byzantium on the Bosphorus as his new capital. The rebuilt city was formally dedicated on 11 May 330 and named Constantinople, the city of Constantine, though it was also called New Rome. The state governed from here, known to historians as the Eastern Roman or Byzantine Empire, lasted 1,123 years.

The city's shield was built a century later. Alarmed by the Gothic sack of Rome in 410, the government of the boy emperor Theodosius II, run by the praetorian prefect Anthemius, raised a new land wall finished around 413. In 447 earthquakes threw down 57 of its 96 towers just as Attila's Huns approached. Some 16,000 workers rebuilt them in about 60 days and added an outer wall and a moat. The Theodosian line, 5.7 km long, is among the oldest surviving defence systems in Europe and part of the Historic Areas of Istanbul, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1985.

Justinian I (527–565) nearly lost his throne before he built the city's greatest monument. In January 532 the Blue and Green factions of the Hippodrome united against him in the Nika riots, and on 13 January the old church of Hagia Sophia was destroyed. After Empress Theodora intervened, Justinian chose to fight. The generals Belisarius and Mundus trapped the crowd in the Hippodrome, and about 30,000 people are said to have died. Work on a new church began at once, and it opened for worship on 27 December 537.

Its designers, Anthemius of Tralles and Isidore of Miletus, were scholars known for their mathematics and mechanics. Their church was probably the largest building in the world when it opened, and UNESCO describes its vast dome as proof of the architectural and decorative skill of the 6th century. Only four years after it opened, disaster struck. Plague appeared in the outer provinces in 541 and reached Constantinople in 542. Procopius reported up to 10,000 deaths a day in the capital; modern historians suggest about 5,000. The cause was Yersinia pestis, the bacterium behind the later Black Death, and outbreaks returned until about 750.

The Umayyad caliphs twice tried to take the city. Chroniclers describe a siege from 674 to 678, broken by Greek fire, a liquid incendiary credited to Kallinikos, a refugee from Syria, which set Arab ships alight. Some historians, notably Marek Jankowiak, argue that the main attack came in 667 to 669 and that the long siege comes from contradictions in the chronicle of Theophanes. The second attempt began on 15 August 717, when Maslama ibn Abd al-Malik arrived with, by one estimate, 80,000 men and 1,800 ships. Leo III turned Greek fire on the fleet, a harsh winter weakened the besiegers, and they withdrew on 15 August 718.

A long quarrel over religious images followed. In 726 Leo III ordered the image of Christ at the entrance of the imperial palace taken down, and in 730 the use of icons was officially prohibited. Persecution was harshest under his successor Constantine V (741–775). The empress Irene restored images at a church council in Nicaea, today's İznik, in 787, but Iconoclasm returned in 815. In 843 the empress Theodora restored icon veneration for good, a victory the Orthodox Church still celebrates as the Feast of Orthodoxy.

Some of the finest painting from after Iconoclasm survives in Cappadocia, where communities carved churches, cells and whole villages into a landscape sculpted by erosion. UNESCO listed Göreme National Park and the Rock Sites of Cappadocia in 1985 as unique evidence of post-Iconoclastic Byzantine art; the listing includes the underground towns of Kaymaklı and Derinkuyu. At the Göreme Open-Air Museum, the Tokalı Church holds a 10th-century cycle of the life of Christ, and the dim Dark Church has kept its 11th-century frescoes in near-original condition.

Basil II (976–1025) was one of the strongest emperors. When the generals Bardas Skleros and Bardas Phokas rebelled, Basil received 6,000 Varangians from Prince Vladimir of Kyiv, who married Basil's sister Anna and accepted Christianity. With them he crossed to Chrysopolis, today's Üsküdar, and crushed the revolt in 989; they became his permanent Varangian Guard. After defeating the Bulgarians at Kleidion in 1014, he blinded the captured army, reportedly about 15,000 men. He also won land in Armenia and Georgia and curbed the military aristocracy and the church as landowners.

The heaviest blow came from Latin Christians. The Fourth Crusade, diverted from the Holy Land, captured and pillaged Constantinople in April 1204, breaking in over the sea walls on 12 April and looting for three days. The crusaders and Venetians founded a Latin Empire in the capital. Byzantine refugees regrouped in Anatolia, where Theodore I Laskaris made Nicaea the base of an empire in exile. Crowned in 1208, he won much of western Anatolia, and his court sponsored a revival of Greek learning.

Nicaea won the race to recover the capital. On 25 July 1261 the general Alexios Strategopoulos took Constantinople, and on 15 August Michael VIII Palaiologos entered the city; his dynasty ruled until 1453. Between 1315 and 1321 the statesman Theodore Metochites filled the Chora church with mosaics and frescoes of the lives of Christ and the Virgin, including an Anastasis, Christ's descent into Hades. Now the Kariye Mosque, it reopened in May 2024 after four years of restoration.

The Seljuks of Rum

How Turkish rulers won Anatolia after Manzikert, built a sultanate at Konya with caravanserais and madrasas, and fell under Mongol rule after 1243.

The Turkish era in Anatolia begins with a battle. On 26 August 1071, at Manzikert, today's Malazgirt, a Seljuk army under Alp Arslan defeated the Byzantines and took the emperor Romanus IV Diogenes prisoner. In the years that followed, Turkish forces conquered most of Anatolia. One of their leaders, Süleyman ibn Kutalmış, held Nicaea, today's İznik, and Nicomedia, today's İzmit, from 1075. In 1077 he made himself independent of the Great Seljuks. His state became the Sultanate of Rum, and Nicaea was its first capital.

The young state soon met the First Crusade. The crusaders left Nicaea on 26 June 1097, and on 1 July Kılıç Arslan I attacked the column led by Bohemond near Dorylaeum. He was driven off when a second crusader column arrived; the number of dead is uncertain. After the crusade Kılıç Arslan moved his capital to Konya. The decisive clash with Byzantium came on 17 September 1176, when Kılıç Arslan II ambushed the army of the emperor Manuel I Komnenos in the mountains west of Konya, at Myriokephalon. The defeat ended Byzantine hopes of driving the Turks out of Anatolia, and, as one historian writes, the land began to be called Turkey.

The sultanate reached its height under Alaeddin Keykubad I, who reigned from 1220 to 1237. In 1221 he took the fortress of Kalon Oros on the south coast and renamed it Alaiye, today's Alanya. He built palaces, mosques and caravanserais. Historians see his reign as a time of economic, commercial and military growth, and credit his strong government with keeping the Mongol threat at bay for a while. Keykubad died in Kayseri on 31 May 1237, killed, according to the same study, in a brutal attack. Under his successor, Keyhüsrev II, the Mongol danger returned.

Long-distance trade crossed the sultanate on its main east-west and north-south roads. Along them the Seljuks built nearly one hundred monumental caravanserais, spaced at intervals. With fortified walls and grand portals, and paid for by charitable endowments called waqfs, they gave travellers safe lodging, medical help and protection for their goods, and showed that the state controlled the trade. The largest is Sultanhanı, commissioned by Keykubad in 1229, about 40 km west of Aksaray on the road to Konya. Covering about 4,900 square metres, it is the largest medieval caravanserai in Türkiye, and it has been on UNESCO's tentative list since 2014.

Konya flourished. In the 13th century, when the Mongols destroyed great centres of learning such as Bukhara, Samarkand and Baghdad, scholars looked for new homes, and the Seljuk capital became one of the centres of Islamic civilisation, busy with building and study. Madrasas, or colleges, were among its finest buildings. The Karatay Madrasa, close to Alaeddin Hill, the old inner castle of Konya, was built in 1251 for the grand vizier Celaleddin Karatay in the reign of Keykavus II. Scholars and sages met there. It is known for its monumental portal and dense tile mosaics, and its closed courtyard refines a plan first seen in Anatolia about a century earlier.

In 1243 the Mongols defeated the Seljuk army at Köse Dağ. After the battle the sultanate fell under Mongol domination, first in its finances, then militarily and finally in its territory. Turkmen groups rose in revolt again and again, for example in 1249, 1256 and 1276. In the most ambitious rising, the Turkmen leader Karamanoğlu Mehmet Bey and a pretender named Siyavush seized the capital, Konya, for a short time. They put Siyavush on the throne and struck coins in his name. Historians know him as Jimri and generally regard him as a false prince.

Konya's most famous resident lived through these troubled years. The poet and mystic Mevlana Celaleddin Rumi died in the city on 17 December 1273. He founded the Mevlevi Sufi brotherhood. A 1977 study of the Mevlevi ceremony describes his teaching as one of tolerance, goodness, charity and awareness through love, looking 'with the same eye on Muslim, Jew and Christian alike'. In 1926 a museum named after him opened in Konya. It tells the history of the Mevlevi dervish lodge, shows art from the Seljuk and Ottoman periods and draws visitors from all over the world.

Mongol overlordship lasted roughly a century after Köse Dağ. The Seljuk sultans remained the principal rulers of central Anatolia, though in the end only in name, and their dynasty came to an obscure end in the early 14th century. By then Anatolia was divided among numerous Turkish principalities, the beyliks. One of them, on the western frontier of the old sultanate, would become the core of the Ottoman state.

The rise of the Ottomans

How Osman's Turkmen principality in north-western Anatolia took Bursa, crossed into Europe, survived defeat by Timur and in 1453 captured Constantinople.

By the early 14th century Anatolia was divided among Turkmen principalities, the beyliks. The ruler of one in the north-west, Osman I, was born about 1258 and belonged to the Kayı branch of the Oğuz Turks. His father, Ertuğrul, had held a principality centred on Söğüt. The word Ottoman comes from ʿUthmān, the Arabic form of Osman's name. Tradition dates the founding of his state to 1299. He died in 1324 or 1326; the sources disagree.

Osman's son Orhan took Bursa on 6 April 1326, though some sources date its capture to 1324. The city gave the principality the means to become a real state with an army, and Orhan declared himself independent of his overlords. Nicaea, today's İznik, fell in 1331. Orhan built mosques, colleges and caravanserais in the conquered towns, above all in Bursa, the first great Ottoman capital, where the tombs of Osman and Orhan stand on a terrace above the city.

In 2014 UNESCO inscribed Bursa and Cumalıkızık: the Birth of the Ottoman Empire on the World Heritage List. Its eight components show how the early sultans built their capital. Orhan, Murad I, Bayezid I, Mehmed I and Murad II each founded a külliye, a complex of mosque, madrasa, bath, public kitchen and tomb, and these complexes set the limits of the city. The nearby village of Cumalıkızık was endowed to provide income for Orhan's complex.

The Ottomans reached Europe through a Byzantine civil war. In 1346 Orhan became the chief ally of the future emperor John VI Cantacuzenus and married his daughter Theodora. From 1354 Orhan's son Süleyman made Gallipoli, on the European side of the Dardanelles, a permanent base for expansion. Orhan's successor, Murad I, pushed into Thrace and in 1361, or 1362 in some accounts, took Adrianople, the second city of the Byzantine Empire. Renamed Edirne, it became the Ottoman centre in Thrace; sources differ on whether it was also the capital before 1413.

The sultans' own infantry were the Janissaries, the yeniçeri or 'new soldiers', an elite standing force first formed under Murad I around 1380. They served as the sultan's household troops and bodyguard. Recruits came through the devşirme, 'the collecting', a periodic levy of boys from Christian peasant families, mostly in the Balkans, who were converted to Islam and trained as soldiers or palace servants.

Murad I also built an imaret, a charitable kitchen and hospice, at İznik for his mother Nilüfer Hatun in 1388. Its plan, an inverted T, is rare in Ottoman architecture. After serving as a warehouse, it was restored in 1960 and opened the same year as the İznik Museum. It shows finds from prehistory to the Ottoman period, among them material from the continuing excavations of the town's tile kilns.

On 28 June 1389 Murad fought the Serbian prince Lazar Hrebeljanović at Kosovo Polje. Both leaders died, the Ottomans won and Serbia collapsed. Murad's son Bayezid I blockaded Constantinople from 1391 to 1398. On 25 September 1396, at Nicopolis, now Nikopol in Bulgaria, he destroyed a crusader army led by King Sigismund of Hungary. The French knights charged rashly and were broken by Bayezid's reserves and a flanking attack by some 5,000 Serbian soldiers.

At the Battle of Ankara on 20 July 1402 the exhausted, thirsty army of Bayezid, called Yıldırım, 'the Thunderbolt', about 85,000 men, faced an estimated 140,000 under Timur, or Tamerlane. Anatolian troops deserted, and Bayezid became the first Ottoman ruler captured by an enemy. He died in captivity in 1403. Timur divided the Ottoman lands among Bayezid's sons, who fought each other for eleven years. Mehmed I, helped by the Byzantine emperor Manuel II, defeated his brother Musa at Çamurlu in 1413 and reunited the state. The capital moved from Bursa, sacked by Timur, to Edirne.

Under Murad II, who reigned from 1421 to 1451 with a break between 1444 and 1446, the Janissaries became perhaps the first infantry force in the world equipped mainly with firearms. Ottoman armies marched to the music of the mehter, the military band, with the kös, a giant kettledrum, the nakkare, the davul bass drum, zil cymbals, the zurna and the boru trumpet. The mehter is thought to be the oldest kind of military marching band.

In June 1444 Murad II made peace at Edirne and abdicated in favour of his 12-year-old son, Mehmed II. A crusade called by Pope Eugene IV and led by János Hunyadi of Transylvania and Władysław III, king of Poland and Hungary, marched on the Ottomans. Murad took command again, and on 10 November he crushed the crusaders at Varna, in today's Bulgaria. Władysław died in a rash attack. The battle ended the efforts of European powers to save Constantinople.

Mehmed II returned to the throne in 1451. In 1452 he built the fortress of Rumeli Hisarı on the European bank of the Bosporus, at its narrowest point, to restrict passage between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean. He set Urban, a Hungarian gunsmith, to arm the fortress and to build cannon powerful enough to bring down the walls of Constantinople. By March 1453 Urban's cannon had been hauled from Edirne to the outskirts of the city.

Mehmed began the siege on 6 April 1453. A chain across the Golden Horn, the inlet that forms the city's harbour, kept the Ottoman fleet out. On the night of 22 April some seventy ships were dragged overland on greased rollers over the hill behind Galata and launched into the Golden Horn. The final assault came before dawn on 29 May 1453. The Ottomans broke through the ancient land wall, and the Byzantine Empire came to an end.

The Ottoman classical age

From Selim I's conquest of Egypt to the Tulip Era, the Ottoman Empire reached the height of its power, with new laws, great mosques and a confident court culture.

Süleyman's father, Selim I, remembered in English as 'the Grim', turned south against the Mamluk sultans of Egypt. His army defeated them at Marj Dabiq, north of Aleppo, on 24 August 1516, and at Raydaniyya, outside Cairo, on 22 January 1517. Syria, Palestine and Egypt passed under Ottoman rule. In Cairo the sharif of Mecca presented Selim with the keys of the holy city, a sign that he was now the leading ruler of the Islamic world and protector of Mecca and Medina. Selim doubled the size of the empire.

His son Süleyman I ruled from 1520 to 1566 and led his armies in person. He took Belgrade in 1521 and the island of Rhodes after a siege in 1522 and 1523. At Mohács in 1526 his army broke the military strength of the kingdom of Hungary. In 1529 he laid siege to Vienna but failed to take it. The siege marked the furthest point of Ottoman expansion in central Europe. Süleyman was still on campaign when he died, in September 1566, near the Hungarian fortress of Szigetvár, which his troops were besieging.

Europeans called him 'the Magnificent'. His own subjects called him Kanuni, 'the Lawgiver', because he changed the organisation of the Ottoman legal system. A kanun was a law issued by the sultan on matters that Islamic religious law, the sharia, did not explicitly cover. Under Süleyman the two bodies of law were brought into closer harmony. His reign is often seen as the high point of Ottoman law, literature, art and architecture.

Hürrem Sultan, known in Europe as Roxelana, was born around 1505 in Rohatyn, then in Poland and now in Ukraine. She was captured, enslaved and taken into the imperial harem, where she was named Hürrem, 'the joyful one'. Breaking with Ottoman tradition, Süleyman married her legally, which freed her. She bore him at least five sons, wrote to foreign rulers, paid for public works and moved the harem into Topkapı Palace. She died in April 1558. Her son followed Süleyman on the throne as Selim II.

At sea the sultan relied on a former corsair. Hayreddin Barbarossa, 'Redbeard', had brought Algeria under Ottoman rule, and in 1533 Süleyman made him admiral in chief. In late September 1538, off Preveza in north-western Greece, he defeated a larger fleet sent by Venice, the Pope and the emperor Charles V under the Genoese admiral Andrea Doria. The victory secured the eastern Mediterranean for the Ottomans. Ottoman control at sea lasted until 7 October 1571, when a Holy League fleet under Don Juan of Austria destroyed the main Ottoman galley fleet at Lepanto.

The builder of the age was Mimar Sinan. Born around 1490 to Christian parents in the village of Ağırnaz, he was taken into Ottoman service through the devşirme and served as a Janissary and military engineer. In 1538 he became chief architect of the empire, a post he held until his death in 1588, and he oversaw the design of several hundred buildings. For Süleyman he built the Süleymaniye complex in Istanbul between 1550 and 1557, on the city's Third Hill. Süleyman, Hürrem and Sinan himself are buried there.

Sinan described the Süleymaniye as his journeyman work. He regarded the Selimiye Mosque in Edirne, built for Selim II between 1568 and 1575, as his most important work. Its single dome, 31.5 metres across, rests on eight pillars over a prayer hall of 45 by 36 metres. With its four tall minarets, the mosque dominates the city skyline. The walls carry İznik tiles from the peak years of their production. Madrasas, a covered market and a library surround the mosque. UNESCO listed the complex as a World Heritage Site in 2011.

Much of the state was run by the sultan's own servants. Under the devşirme, officials took boys from Christian peasant families, mostly in the Balkans, converted them to Islam and trained them for the palace or for the Janissaries, the yeniçeri or 'new soldiers'. Legally the sultan's slaves, the Janissaries were his elite infantry and bodyguard, and also served as police, firefighters and garrison troops. Non-Muslim subjects lived in millets, self-governing religious communities such as the Greek Orthodox, the Armenians and the Jews. Each had its own laws and a religious head who answered to the government for taxes and order.

In the 16th century the potters of İznik made fritware tiles painted with stylised flowers in blue, white, red and green for the imperial mosques and palaces of Istanbul. In 1554, or 1551 by some accounts, two merchants from Aleppo and Damascus opened the first recorded coffee house in Istanbul, in the Tahtakale quarter. Coffee houses soon spread across the city as places to talk, read and share news. UNESCO added Turkish coffee culture and tradition to its list of intangible heritage in 2013.

The Tulip Era ran from the Treaty of Passarowitz of 21 July 1718 to a revolt in Istanbul on 28 September 1730. It takes its name from the tulip craze at the court of Ahmed III and his grand vizier and son-in-law, Nevşehirli Damat İbrahim Pasha. It was a time of luxury, poetry, new building and growing interest in Europe. In 1727 İbrahim Müteferrika set up the first officially approved Ottoman Turkish printing press. After defeats against Safavid Persia, a revolt led by Patrona Halil, a former seaman and Janissary, brought the regime down.

Decline and reform

Between the failed siege of Vienna in 1683 and the Balkan Wars of 1912–1913, the empire lost land in war and tried to remake its army, laws and government.

The long retreat began outside Vienna. In July 1683 the grand vizier Kara Mustafa Pasha laid siege to the city with an army that far outnumbered its defenders. The walls held, and on 12 September a relief army led by the Polish king Jan Sobieski routed the Ottomans in the Battle of Vienna on the slopes of the Kahlenberg. Kara Mustafa fell back to Belgrade, where he was strangled on the sultan's orders on 25 December 1683. The defeat ended the age of Ottoman expansion in Europe and opened a sixteen-year war against a Holy League of Austria, Poland, Venice and Russia.

The war went badly for the Ottomans. On 11 September 1697 Prince Eugene of Savoy crushed the army of Sultan Mustafa II at Zenta. The Treaty of Karlowitz, signed on 26 January 1699, handed most of Hungary and Transylvania to Austria, Podolia to Poland, and the Peloponnese and most of Dalmatia to Venice. For the first time the Ottomans negotiated with a coalition of European states, accepted mediation by neutral powers and admitted defeat. Austria became the dominant power in east-central Europe.

In the 18th century Russia pushed south towards the Black Sea. The Russo-Turkish War of 1768–1774 ended with the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca, signed on 21 July 1774 in a village in today's Bulgaria. It ended undisputed Ottoman control of the Black Sea. Russia gained Azov, Kerch and other fortresses, and the Crimean Khanate became independent, bound to the sultan only in religious matters. Russia annexed Crimea in 1783. The treaty also allowed Russia to build an Orthodox church in Istanbul, and Russia went on to claim the role of protector of the sultan's Orthodox Christian subjects.

Some sultans concluded that the army itself had to change. Selim III, who reigned from 1789 to 1807, launched the Nizam-ı Cedid, or 'New Order', a programme of reforms on Western lines. He created a corps of regular infantry trained and drilled in the European manner, reorganised the artillery and the fleet, and opened military and naval schools to teach gunnery, fortification and navigation. The Janissaries and many religious scholars opposed him, and in 1805 provincial notables in the Balkans joined them. In 1807 a mutiny of auxiliary troops forced Selim to abandon the reforms and cost him his throne.

Mahmud II, sultan from 1808 to 1839, moved more carefully. For almost twenty years he isolated the Janissaries and won over religious leaders. In June 1826, when the Janissaries revolted against his military reforms, he had their barracks in Istanbul bombarded. Thousands died in the fighting and in the executions that followed, and the corps was abolished. Turkish historians call the event the Vaka-i Hayriye, the 'Auspicious Incident'. Mahmud replaced the Janissaries with a European-style army and a military academy, clearing the way for deeper reform.

Mahmud's sons Abdülmecid I and Abdülaziz presided over the Tanzimat, or 'Reorganisation', from 1839 to 1876. On 3 November 1839 the Noble Edict of the Rose Chamber, the Hatt-ı Şerif of Gülhane, promised security of life, property and honour to all subjects, whatever their religion, along with a standard system of taxation and fairer conscription. A second edict, of 18 February 1856, promised equality in education, government posts and the courts regardless of creed. Both came when the empire needed European support, first against Muhammad Ali of Egypt and then after the Crimean War.

That war grew out of Russian demands to protect the sultan's Orthodox subjects. After a Russian squadron destroyed an Ottoman fleet at Sinop on 30 November 1853, Britain and France declared war on Russia in March 1854 and fought alongside the Ottomans in the Crimean War. The allies took Sevastopol in 1855, and the Treaty of Paris of 30 March 1856 guaranteed the empire's territorial integrity. The war also brought new technology. In 1855 the Ottoman government, the British and French armies and British investors built the first telegraph lines in the empire, linking Istanbul to the European network.

Reformers now wanted limits on the sultan's power. On 23 December 1876 Abdülhamid II proclaimed the first Ottoman constitution, the Kanun-i Esasi, partly to head off foreign intervention after the violent suppression of a Bulgarian uprising. An elected parliament met in March 1877. War with Russia followed, and in February 1878 Abdülhamid dismissed parliament and suspended the constitution. For the next 30 years he ruled from Yıldız Palace in Istanbul, supported by a system of secret police.

Opposition gathered in the Committee of Union and Progress, the main organisation of the Young Turks. In July 1908 its revolt forced Abdülhamid to restore the constitution and recall parliament, and the Young Turk Revolution ended three decades of personal rule. In 1913 the Committee took control of the government. By then war had returned. On 8 October 1912 the states of the Balkan League attacked the empire, and the Treaty of London of 30 May 1913 took almost all its remaining land in Europe, including Macedonia and Albania. In the Second Balkan War that summer the Ottomans won back Edirne, which Bulgarian troops had captured in March.

War and the end of empire

How the Ottoman Empire lost the First World War, the deportation and mass killing of the Armenians, and the War of Independence that ended at Lausanne in 1923.

The empire entered the First World War as Germany's ally. On 2 August 1914 the Ottoman and German governments agreed an alliance, and on 29 October the Ottoman Empire officially joined the war as one of the Central Powers. In November the sultan proclaimed a jihad against the Allies, and Ottoman and German propaganda called the Muslims of India, Egypt and other Allied colonies to holy war.

The first great offensive ended in disaster. In late December 1914 Enver Pasha sent tens of thousands of poorly clothed and poorly equipped soldiers across mountain passes more than 2,000 metres high in the Caucasus, hoping to capture a strategic railhead and inflict a heavy defeat on the Russian army. The campaign around Sarıkamış lasted about two weeks. Some 50,000 Ottoman soldiers died, about a quarter of them from frostbite and exposure.

In 1915 the Allies struck at the capital. The Gallipoli campaign was a British-led attempt to force a way through the Dardanelles, take the Gallipoli peninsula and occupy Constantinople. When Allied troops landed in April 1915, Mustafa Kemal commanded the 19th Division, the main reserve of the Ottoman Fifth Army, and his men opposed the landing of the Australian and New Zealand troops known as the Anzacs. The fighting on the peninsula went on into 1916 and ended in failure for the Allies.

Behind the fronts, the war brought mass death to the empire's Armenians. In 1915 and 1916 the Ottoman government deported its Armenian subjects, and massacres accompanied the deportations. Estimates made between 1915 and 1923 most often put the number who died in these two years at about one million. By these calculations some 600,000 died in 1915 in the deportations and massacres, and around 400,000 died in 1916 in camps in Syria and Mesopotamia, from starvation and disease and in the violence that followed when the camps were broken up. The historian Raymond Kévorkian puts the number of Armenian dead at up to 1.5 million. After the war there were attempts to bring those responsible to justice.

Except in Turkey and among pro-Turkish commentators, it is generally accepted that what happened to the Armenians in 1915 and 1916 was genocide. By 2019 more than 20 countries had recognised the Armenian genocide, while Turkey denies it. Turkish history textbooks present the deportations as a wartime relocation under a law on dispatch and settlement, a response to Armenian revolts that was not intended to exterminate the Armenians. Other Christians also suffered in the empire's last decades, when millions of people, mostly Christians, died, were forcibly moved or were expelled. Many writers now describe the fate of Ottoman Greeks and Assyrians between 1915 and 1923 as genocide too. Andrekos Varnava argues that the term fits the Armenian case of 1915–1916 but not the Greek one.

In the Arab provinces Britain found allies in the Hashemites of the Hejaz, the region around Mecca and Medina, led by Sharif Hussein. In June 1916 they rose in revolt and overcame the Ottoman garrison in Mecca, though Medina held out until 1919. Britain, and to a lesser extent France, provided supplies, money and advisers. The Hashemites hoped to end Ottoman rule and create Arab states under their own leadership.

The empire's war ended with the Armistice of Mudros, signed on 30 October 1918. On the morning of 13 November a fleet of British, French, Italian and Greek battleships sailed to Istanbul and dropped anchor without meeting resistance. The historian Ari Şekeryan sees that day as the beginning of the end of the empire. In May 1919 Greece, with Allied permission, landed troops at İzmir, a port city with a large Greek population.

Resistance took shape in Anatolia. On 19 May 1919 Mustafa Kemal landed at Samsun on an official mission to eastern Anatolia. In July and August the Erzurum Congress elected him its head and drafted the text that became the National Pact, which held that all Ottoman lands inhabited by Turks when the Mudros armistice was signed must stay undivided. The Sivas Congress ratified it in September. On 23 April 1920 a new parliament, the Grand National Assembly, opened in Ankara in a one-storey building of local andesite stone. The assembly met there until October 1924.

The Allies set out their terms in the Treaty of Sèvres of 10 August 1920. Turkey was to renounce all rights over Arab Asia and North Africa and accept an independent Armenia and an autonomous Kurdistan. Greece would gain a presence in eastern Thrace and on the Anatolian west coast, in terms that pointed to the eventual loss of İzmir. An Allied commission would oversee the demilitarised straits, and Europeans would control Ottoman finances. The nationalists rejected the treaty. In the east their troops attacked Armenia in September 1920 and took Kars. The Treaty of Kars, signed with Soviet Armenia, Azerbaijan and Georgia on 13 October 1921, fixed the border that Türkiye and Armenia still share.

In the south, French forces held Cilicia, including Adana. After defeats by the nationalists in 1920 and 1921, France signed the Treaty of Ankara on 20 October 1921. It agreed to evacuate Cilicia and in effect recognised the Grand National Assembly as the sovereign power in Turkey. In the west the Greek army advanced into Anatolia in January 1921 and was repelled at İnönü that month and again in April. In July it attacked once more and pushed towards Ankara, until the Battle of the Sakarya River, fought from 23 August to 13 September 1921, halted the advance.

On 26 August 1922 Mustafa Kemal launched the Great Offensive from Kocatepe. At Dumlupınar the Greek army was decisively defeated by 30 August, a victory Türkiye marks each year as Victory Day. The Greeks were pushed back to the sea, and on 9 September Turkish troops entered İzmir. Four days later a fire broke out that by 22 September had destroyed the Greek and Armenian quarters, while the Muslim and Jewish quarters escaped. Many Greeks and Armenians were killed, and estimates of the dead vary widely. Responsibility is disputed. Some sources blame Turks, others Greeks or Armenians. The historian Norman Naimark sees the sparing of the Turkish quarter as a sign of Turkish responsibility, while others argue that burning the city ran against Turkish interests.

The Armistice of Mudanya of 11 October 1922 ended the fighting with the Allies, who accepted the Turkish return to Istanbul and eastern Thrace. On 1 November the assembly abolished the sultanate, and sixteen days later Mehmed VI boarded a British warship and left for Malta. The Treaty of Lausanne, signed on 24 July 1923 by Turkey on one side and by Britain, France, Italy, Japan, Greece, Romania and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes on the other, replaced Sèvres and recognised the borders of the new Turkish state. The Allies dropped their demands for Kurdish autonomy and Armenian territory, and the straits were demilitarised.

Greece and Turkey had already agreed a compulsory exchange of populations in a convention signed at Lausanne on 30 January 1923. The Greeks of Istanbul and the Muslims of Western Thrace were exempted. Britannica estimates that about 1.3 million Greeks left Turkey and some 400,000 Turks came from Greece. Other counts give about 1.2 million Greek Orthodox Christians and between 355,000 and 400,000 Muslims. Tens of thousands of destitute Greek refugees had already fled in 1922, which Britannica describes as the end of a 2,500-year Greek presence in Asia Minor.

Building a new republic

How Mustafa Kemal and the Grand National Assembly turned the Ottoman heartland into a secular nation-state, from the proclamation of 1923 to the end of the Second World War.

On 29 October 1923 the Grand National Assembly amended the constitution and proclaimed the Republic of Turkey. The deputies elected Mustafa Kemal, who had led the national forces in the War of Independence, as its first president by a unanimous vote. Two weeks earlier, on 13 October, they had declared Ankara the capital. The old imperial city of Istanbul gave way to a modest town in central Anatolia, far from the coasts that had been under foreign control. The republic would be governed from the heartland its army had defended.

On 3 March 1924 the assembly abolished the caliphate, a title the Ottoman sultans had held since 1517, and expelled the last caliph, Abdülmecid II, with all members of the former dynasty. Mustafa Kemal argued that a caliphate could not exist inside a republic. A state Directorate of Religious Affairs replaced the Ministry of Sharia and Pious Foundations. A new constitution followed on 20 April 1924. Under it the nation, no longer Islam or the caliph, was the source of law.

On 11 February 1925 Sheikh Said, a Zaza Kurdish sheikh of the Naqshbandi order, launched a revolt in the village of Piran in southeast Anatolia. Historians still debate how far it was driven by Kurdish nationalism and how far by anger at the end of the caliphate and the secular reforms. The army crushed it with troops and air raids. A Law on the Maintenance of Order, passed on 4 March, revived the Independence Tribunals and was used to silence the opposition Progressive Republican Party. Sheikh Said and other leaders were hanged on 29 June 1925. That November the dervish lodges and religious orders were closed.

Reform then reached into daily life. The Hat Law of 25 November 1925 declared the hat the general headdress of the Turkish nation and required men to wear western-style hats in public. It was aimed at the fez, which many people regarded as a religious symbol, and protests in Erzurum led to martial law. On 17 February 1926 the assembly adopted a new civil code modelled on the Swiss one. Civil marriage became compulsory, polygamy was banned, and the law treated women and men as equals for the first time.

The deepest cultural break came with writing. Law No. 1353, passed on 1 November 1928, ended the use of the Arabic-based Ottoman script and introduced a Latin alphabet for Turkish. From 1 January 1929 the new letters were compulsory in all public communication. Literacy was very low, and reformers argued that the old script suited the sounds of Turkish poorly. Mustafa Kemal went into the countryside with chalk and a blackboard to show people the new letters and explain how to pronounce them.

Women gained political rights in stages. A municipal law of 3 April 1930 allowed them to vote and stand in local elections. A constitutional amendment on 5 December 1934 extended both rights to parliamentary elections. In the general election of 8 February 1935, 17 women entered the assembly, and a by-election soon brought the number to 18, or 4.6 per cent of the seats. On 21 June 1934 a Surname Law had required every citizen to take a family name. On 24 November the assembly gave Mustafa Kemal the surname Atatürk, “Father of the Turks”, and reserved it for him alone.

The ideas behind the reforms were summed up in six principles, often called the Six Arrows: republicanism, nationalism, populism, statism, secularism and revolutionism, also translated as reformism. They entered the programme of the Republican People's Party in 1931 and the constitution in 1937. Statism meant state-led development through investment banks, monopolies, state factories and planning. Secularism meant a common secular system of education and family law. People's Houses in provincial towns and Village Institutes in the countryside spread the new national culture.

The state's authority was also imposed by force. In Dersim, the mountainous region that is now the province of Tunceli, the army conducted military operations between March 1937 and September 1938 against what the government called a rebellion, using artillery and aircraft. The victims were mainly Alevi Kurds, among them many thousands of civilians. In 2011 Prime Minister Recep Tayyip Erdoğan cited official documents of the time recording 13,806 people killed and 11,683 displaced, and offered an apology on behalf of the state. Other estimates range up to 70,000 dead. The events are widely known today as the Dersim massacre.

After a long illness Atatürk died in Istanbul at 9.05 in the morning on 10 November 1938, in Dolmabahçe Palace. The next day the assembly unanimously elected İsmet İnönü, his former prime minister, as president. In 1939 the republic gained territory in the south. The Sanjak of Alexandretta, part of French-mandated Syria, had become the short-lived Hatay State in September 1938. On 29 June 1939 its assembly voted to join Turkey, and in July it became the Turkish province of Hatay. Syria did not recognise the change, and the question remains disputed.

When the Second World War began in 1939, İnönü kept Turkey neutral. Ankara sought arms from both the Allies and the Axis and signed a treaty of friendship with Germany. It stopped selling chromite ore to Germany in April 1944 and, after the Soviet army reached Bulgaria, broke off relations that August. On 23 February 1945 the assembly voted unanimously to declare war on Germany and Japan. The declaration was largely a formality that allowed Turkey to join the new United Nations. No Turkish troops went into action, and the country's economy and cities came through the war intact.

Türkiye since 1945

Coups, elections and crises since 1945, from NATO and Cyprus to Erdoğan's presidency, the 2023 earthquakes, the Istanbul mayor's arrest and a peace process with the Kurdistan Workers' Party.

The one-party era ended after the Second World War. In 1946 Celal Bayar and Adnan Menderes, expelled from the Republican People's Party (CHP) the year before, founded the Democrat Party. In the election of 14 May 1950 it won 55 per cent of the vote and 416 of 487 seats. Bayar became president and Menderes prime minister. From October 1950 about 4,500 Turkish soldiers fought under the United Nations Command in Korea, and on 18 February 1952 Turkey joined NATO.

Menderes' decade in power ended with a military coup on 27 May 1960. After trials on the island of Yassıada, Menderes was hanged on İmralı island on 17 September 1961, a day after two of his ministers. The army intervened again on 12 March 1971, when the chief of the general staff handed Prime Minister Süleyman Demirel a memorandum demanding a strong government to end street and campus violence. Demirel resigned the same day.

Cyprus became the most lasting foreign-policy dispute. On 15 July 1974 Greek Cypriot nationalists backed by the junta in Athens overthrew the island's president, Archbishop Makarios, seeking union with Greece. On 20 July Turkish troops landed in the north. Turkey presents its action as a response to the coup and to attacks on Turkish Cypriots since 1963, while Greek Cypriots regard it as an invasion. By 18 August Turkish forces held about 36 per cent of the island. In 1983 the north declared itself the Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus, which only Turkey recognises.

On 12 September 1980 the armed forces under General Kenan Evren seized power again. According to Anadolu Agency, some 650,000 people were detained, 230,000 were tried, 50 were executed and 299 died from torture or prison conditions. A new constitution was approved by 91 per cent of voters in 1982. Turgut Özal, the main architect of the “24 January decisions” of 1980 that opened up the economy, led his new Motherland Party to a parliamentary majority in 1983. He served as prime minister until 1989 and as president until 1993.

On 15 August 1984 the Kurdistan Workers' Party (PKK) raided police posts in Eruh and Şemdinli, the start of a full-scale armed insurgency in the south-east. The conflict is commonly estimated to have killed between 30,000 and 40,000 people, although the International Crisis Group notes that many death claims cannot be verified independently. In December 1999 European Union leaders meeting in Helsinki granted Turkey candidate status, and accession negotiations opened on 3 October 2005.

The Justice and Development Party (AKP), co-founded by Recep Tayyip Erdoğan in 2001, won the election of 3 November 2002 with 34 per cent of the vote and 363 of 550 seats. Erdoğan was at first barred from office. After the ban was overturned he won a by-election in Siirt and became prime minister in March 2003. He held the post until 2014, when he became president.

In late May 2013 a sit-in in Gezi Park, beside Istanbul's Taksim Square, opposed a plan to build a replica Ottoman barracks there. After police cleared the park on 30 May, protests spread to more than 80 cities, with an estimated 2 to 3.5 million participants. They voiced anger over urban planning, limits on alcohol sales and what they saw as Erdoğan's growing authoritarianism. In 2019 prosecutors brought new charges over the protests, which Human Rights Watch called baseless.

Engineering changed the Bosphorus. The first Bosphorus Bridge opened on 30 October 1973 and the Fatih Sultan Mehmet Bridge on 3 July 1988. On 29 October 2013 the Marmaray rail tunnel opened beneath the strait, after repeated delays caused by archaeological finds. At Yenikapı excavators uncovered a 4th-century Byzantine harbour and about 35 ships of the 7th to 10th centuries. A third suspension bridge, the Yavuz Sultan Selim, opened on 26 August 2016.

Six weeks before that opening, on 15 July 2016, an attempted military coup left 246 people dead and 2,194 wounded. The government blamed followers of Fethullah Gülen, a cleric living in Pennsylvania, and sought his extradition, although a 2016 analysis noted that the government had produced no clear evidence to support the request. The first Bosphorus Bridge now carries the date in its official name, the 15 July Martyrs' Bridge. Under a state of emergency, more than 130,000 judges, soldiers, police officers, teachers and other public servants were dismissed, a purge that a study in the Human Rights Law Review finds incompatible with the European Convention on Human Rights.

In a 2017 referendum voters narrowly approved an executive presidency in place of the parliamentary system. It came into force with the elections of 24 June 2018, the first in which voters chose a president and a parliament on the same day. Erdoğan won 52.5 per cent and became the first executive president, and in the parliamentary vote his AKP took 42.4 per cent and its far-right ally 11.2 per cent. A currency crisis followed. Between 2 and 13 August the dollar rose from 5.09 to 7.24 lira, according to the economist John Taskinsoy.

The civil war in Syria brought millions of refugees to Türkiye, which for more than a decade has hosted one of the largest refugee populations in the world. At the end of 2024 some 2.9 million Syrians lived there under temporary protection, according to the United Nations refugee agency, UNHCR. After the Assad government fell in December 2024, many began to go back. UNHCR reported that around 560,000 Syrians returned voluntarily from Türkiye within a year.

On 6 February 2023 a magnitude 7.8 earthquake struck south of Kahramanmaraş, and less than 12 hours later a second powerful shock hit north-west of the city. About 280,000 buildings collapsed or were severely damaged. Hatay, with about 21,900 dead, and Kahramanmaraş, with about 12,600, were the worst-hit provinces. In Türkiye and Syria together the earthquakes killed more than 50,000 people and injured about 107,000.

Three months later Erdoğan faced Kemal Kılıçdaroğlu, leader of the CHP. In the first round on 14 May Erdoğan took 49.5 per cent and Kılıçdaroğlu 44.9, so neither won outright, while the AKP and its allies kept a parliamentary majority with 323 of 600 seats. The nationalist Sinan Oğan, who had won 5.2 per cent, then endorsed Erdoğan. In the run-off on 28 May Erdoğan won 52.2 per cent to 47.8 and secured five more years in office.

The opposition gained ground in the local elections of 31 March 2024. The CHP's Ekrem İmamoğlu held Istanbul with just over 51 per cent and Mansur Yavaş held Ankara with just over 60. Al Jazeera called the result the biggest defeat for Erdoğan and the AKP in more than two decades. Nationally the CHP won 37.8 per cent of the vote to just under 35.5 per cent for the AKP, and it led in 36 of the 81 provinces.

On 19 March 2025 İmamoğlu, seen as a key rival to Erdoğan, was detained and later jailed on corruption and terrorism charges. The arrest set off what Al Jazeera described as the largest anti-government protests in a decade, and nearly 1,900 people were detained. The CHP called the arrest a “coup”. The government rejects claims that the case is politically motivated and insists that the judiciary is independent.

In February 2025 the PKK's imprisoned founder, Abdullah Öcalan, called on the group to lay down its arms and dissolve itself. After a congress in northern Iraq, the PKK announced on 12 May that it would dissolve and end its armed struggle, and Erdoğan hailed the move as serving “peace and fraternity”. On 11 July about 20 to 30 fighters burned their weapons in a cauldron at a ceremony in Sulaymaniyah, in Iraq's Kurdish region. On 10 August 2026 parliament approved a law by 468 votes to 88 that offers a conditional pardon to thousands of PKK members who lay down their weapons, without a general amnesty.

In May 2026 a court annulled the CHP's 2023 leadership election, removing Özgür Özel as party leader and naming his predecessor, Kılıçdaroğlu, as interim leader. In July Özel and 90 other deputies who had broken with the CHP launched the New Party, which he said marked “the hope of a new beginning” in the effort to unseat Erdoğan. Analysts said the split could help Erdoğan and the AKP by dividing the opposition.

Visiting history today

A traveller's guide to the places in this story, region by region, from Istanbul, Troy and Ephesus to Hattusa, Cappadocia, Adana and Göbekli Tepe.

Almost every chapter of this story left something a traveller can still see. The atlas below pins every place named on these pages. Choose a pin for a short note, or filter the pins by era. The notes below group the main sites into journeys by region. Many are on UNESCO's World Heritage List, from Göbekli Tepe, inscribed in 2018, to Göreme and the rock sites of Cappadocia, inscribed in 1985. Others wait on UNESCO's tentative list, among them Anazarbos in Cilicia and the rock tombs of the kings of Pontus at Amasya.

Istanbul deserves several days. UNESCO's listing of the Historic Areas of Istanbul names the Hippodrome of Constantine, Hagia Sophia and the Süleymaniye Mosque among the city's masterpieces. Justinian's Hagia Sophia opened for worship on 27 December 537, after the Nika riots had destroyed the older church on the site. Since 15 January 2024 foreign visitors have paid €25 and follow a route through the upper galleries, where the Byzantine mosaics are, while worship continues on the ground floor. Sinan's Süleymaniye was founded in 1550 and inaugurated in 1557. Its complex held a hospital, a soup kitchen, schools, a bath, a caravanserai, four madrasas and a medical school, and the tombs of Süleyman and his family stand in its grounds.

Beyond the old centre, the Chora, now the Kariye Mosque, reopened in May 2024 after four years of restoration. Its mosaics and frescoes were commissioned by the statesman Theodore Metochites between 1315 and 1321. Beside the Bosphorus stands Dolmabahçe Palace, where Atatürk died at 9.05 in the morning on 10 November 1938. Since 30 October 1973 the Bosphorus Bridge has joined Europe and Asia across the strait, the first bridge to do so, with a main span of 1,074 metres.

West of Istanbul, Edirne holds the building Sinan counted as his most important work, the Selimiye Mosque, built for Selim II between 1568 and 1575. Its single dome is 31.5 metres across, its walls carry İznik tiles, and UNESCO listed the complex in 2011. Further south, on the Gallipoli peninsula beside the Dardanelles, Mustafa Kemal commanded the 19th Division against the Anzac landing in April 1915. Troy lies close by, a World Heritage Site since 1998. The walls of Troy VI and the remains of the later cities are still visible on the mound, and finds from the excavations are shown in the nearby Troy Museum.

Down the Aegean coast, Pergamon rises above today's Bergama, with temples, theatres, a gymnasium and a library set into the slopes of its hill. UNESCO inscribed it in 2014. Further south, Ephesus, which UNESCO calls an outstanding example of a Roman port city, keeps Hadrian's Temple, the Library of Celsus of 110 CE and the Great Theatre. The frieze of its Great Altar is far away in Berlin, where the Pergamonmuseum is closed for renovation until 2027. Inland, east of Manisa, the Lydian capital of Sardis and the royal burial mounds of Bin Tepe became Türkiye's 22nd World Heritage Site on 12 July 2025. İznik, the old Nicaea, is on the tentative list, and its museum occupies the imaret that Murad I built for his mother Nilüfer Hatun in 1388.

In Ankara, which the Grand National Assembly declared the capital on 13 October 1923, two monuments tell the story of the Republic. The first building of the Assembly in Ulus, used from 23 April 1920 to 15 October 1924, is now the Museum of the War of Independence. On a hill above the city stands Anıtkabir, Atatürk's mausoleum, built between 1944 and 1953 to a design by Emin Onat and Ahmet Orhan Arda. His remains were moved there on 10 November 1953. South-west of Ankara, near Polatlı, lies Gordion, the Phrygian capital, inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 2023.

In the Hittite heartland, Hattusa, beside the village of Boğazkale, keeps its Lions' Gate and King's Gate, its temples and royal residences, and more than 8 kilometres of walls. About 2 kilometres away, the open-air sanctuary of Yazılıkaya shows two processions of gods converging on the storm god Teshub and the goddess Hebat. Also in Çorum province, Alacahöyük gave up the Royal Tombs of about 2500 to 2000 BCE. Further south, Kültepe, about 20 kilometres north-east of Kayseri, is the mound of Kanesh, where Assyrian merchants kept their clay archives.

The mound of Çatalhöyük holds eighteen levels of Neolithic settlement from 7400 to 6200 BCE, with houses entered through their roofs. Konya became the Seljuk capital after the First Crusade. On the old road east from Konya towards Aksaray stands Sultanhanı, commissioned by Keykubad I in 1229. It covers about 4,900 square metres and is the largest medieval caravanserai in Türkiye. Beyond it lies Cappadocia, where the World Heritage listing covers the painted cave churches of Göreme, among them the Tokalı Church and the Dark Church, and the underground towns of Kaymaklı and Derinkuyu.

On the Mediterranean, Alanya takes its name from Alaiye, the name Keykubad I gave the fortress of Kalon Oros after taking it in 1221. Further east, in Adana, the Roman Stone Bridge over the Seyhan has belonged to pedestrians since 2007. North-east of the city lie the ruins of Anazarbos, which a 2026 study counts among the largest archaeological complexes of Cilicia, and in Osmaniye province the colonnaded street of Hierapolis-Kastabala and the Karatepe-Aslantaş Open-Air Museum with Azatiwada's inscriptions.

In the south-east, Göbekli Tepe stands in the Germuş mountains, and its excavation has been run from the start with the Şanlıurfa Museum. Further north, on the summit of Nemrut Dağ, 2,134 metres high near Adıyaman, King Antiochus I of Commagene surrounded his burial mound of stone chips with colossal seated statues of himself and his gods. UNESCO inscribed Nemrut Dağ in 1987.

Some of the most famous finds from these sites are far from home. Most of Schliemann's Priam's Treasure from Troy is in the Pushkin State Museum of Fine Arts in Moscow, and the Pergamon Altar is in Berlin. Fragments of the Hittite copy of the peace treaty with Egypt are in Istanbul, Ankara, Berlin and St Petersburg, and a copper replica given by Turkey in 1970 hangs outside the Security Council chamber of the United Nations in New York. The nearly 25,000 Boğazköy tablets from the Hittite royal archive are on UNESCO's Memory of the World register.
