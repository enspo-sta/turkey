Frame rate · Hotrod Outdoor Alaska Fishing

Frame Rate, Same Picture

Four changes cut the work the processor does to draw each frame, and leave every pixel as it was. Draw calls fell 14–18%, the processor’s drawing time fell 5%, and the same moment drawn with the changes on and off matched pixel for pixel in all ten views tested.

The gain is real but small. On this test machine, the whole frame came out 0.14 ms faster, which is within the noise. Whatever more is needed on the phone has to come from work that changes the picture, or from what a trace on the iPhone itself shows. Nothing here was run on a real iPhone.

The request

“Still too low fps. Optimise without worsening graphics.”

This round came straight after the fix for the lag at the start of a game. The target is the iPhone. The rule was that the picture must not get worse, so every change here was required to leave the picture identical, and was checked pixel by pixel.

Profile

Where the time goes

The game was profiled on the high setting at an iPhone’s landscape size (844 by 390 points, two pixels per point), with the graphics work drained before every frame so the browser’s own drawing would not be counted against JavaScript.

Part of the frame (as shipped)Milliseconds a frame
three.js itself: sorting what to draw, setting up each draw call, sending uniforms8.14
Graphics calls under three.js (vertex arrays, uniforms, buffers, textures)about 2.4, included in the frame below
The game’s own code (trees round you, map, compass, animals, floating things)about 2
The whole measured frame10.85

Most of the processor’s time per frame goes to the cost of each draw call, not to the game. The scene holds only 705 objects. A frame at the landing makes 268 draw calls:

  • 120 for the view;
  • 109 for the sun’s shadow map, which has two cascades;
  • 22 for the water’s reflection;
  • 17 for the finish and the screen.

On the road the view alone takes 321. Every object’s place in the world was also worked out twice a frame: once for the reflection and once for the view. That cost 0.586 ms a frame in the development build.

The adaptive resolution lowers the pixel count when frames run slow. When that does not bring the frame rate up, the limit is likely the processor rather than the graphics chip. So this round went after the processor’s per-draw cost.

The changes

What changed

1. Shadow casters drawn together (src/world/shadowbatch.js)

Everything that casts a shadow is drawn into the shadow map with the same depth-only material. Two kinds of parts are now drawn there together, one stand-in mesh per side the material casts with:

  • the still parts of each site (the landing, the observatory, the pit stop, the tors, the solar system walk, the Tesla memorial and the scientists’ statues), grouped in cells of 100 metres;
  • the parts of each car that keep still on it (the hot rod, the race car and the boat).

Each part keeps its own vertices. Each part is placed by its own matrix, worked out the way three.js works it out for a mesh of its own (the light’s view times the part’s place, in double precision, then rounded once to 32 bits). The matrices are read from a small floating-point texture. So the shadow map gets the same numbers to the bit, in fewer calls.

Before each shadow map is drawn, every part is checked as three.js would check it: shown, in the view’s layers, and casting this frame. A part that is not casting, for example one too small to see, is left out of its stand-in for that frame. A part that has moved on its own, such as a turning wheel, leaves the stand-in for good and casts by itself again.

Only plain casters are merged. Parts with cut-out textures, displacement, clipping, skinning, morphing or a custom depth material are left as they were.

An earlier version placed every vertex in the world ahead of time. Its picture was the same to the eye, but the depth values rounded differently and the self-shadowing on the observatory dome shifted. That version was replaced before release.

2. Far ground drawn four chunks at a time (src/world/terrain.js)

Ground chunks at the two coarsest levels of detail are drawn as one mesh when the whole square of 2 by 2 chunks is at the same level. The triangles are the same, copied as they are, and the calls are a quarter. Near the player, where chunks change level often, nothing changed.

3. Grass that cannot be seen skips its work (src/world/grass.js)

Two kinds of tuft now leave the vertex shader early and send the GPU nothing to draw:

  • a tuft with nothing to show (no grass at that spot, or past the fade);
  • a tuft whose every part lies more than 4 metres outside one side of the view.

Before, the first kind collapsed to a point and the second was clipped away. Either way, neither drew a pixel. The 4-metre margin covers the tallest tuft bent by the strongest gust.

4. One matrix update a frame instead of two (src/game.js, src/world/water.js)

The water’s reflection is now drawn last in the world update. It already works out every object’s place as it is drawn, and nothing moves between then and the view, so the view reuses those places.

The three updates that used to run after the reflection only change what they draw themselves, none of which is in the reflection:

  • the distant puffs and waterfalls;
  • the floating things;
  • the grass.

A matrix-update counter showed one full update a frame instead of two: 0.37 ms against 0.89 ms, measured while another job shared the machine.

The picture

The same picture, pixel for pixel

A new tool, tools/same-frame.mjs, brings the game to a moment at each of ten views. Nothing moves while it draws that moment seven times:

  1. with every change on;
  2. on again, to show that the drawing repeats exactly;
  3. with each of the four changes off in turn (four drawings);
  4. with all of them off.

Every picture is read back and compared pixel by pixel with the first. The views were drawn at 640 by 360 on the high setting, with the finish, the sun’s shadows and the water’s reflection.

Would the check see a small change?

The observatory dome was moved up by a tenth of a millimetre and the moment drawn again:

  • night view: 788 pixels changed;
  • day view: 149 pixels changed;
  • landing (the dome is not in view): no pixels changed.

A change far smaller than any of the four optimisations would make shows up. A change of one part in a million to the shadow offset showed nothing, because it is below the precision of a depth value.

Why two separate builds are not compared

The first check loaded the old and new builds one after the other, with every random number seeded alike, and compared their pictures. The same build against itself still differed. The game’s own loop ran on the title screen for as long as loading took, and time-budgeted work went differently on a busy machine. With the loop held and the clock stopped, three things still moved between loads:

  • the fish swimming in the water, placed while the sounds are still made in the background;
  • a few particles: up to 1,952 pixels in one view, and 0 in two views;
  • the seeded random numbers themselves. three.js draws four random numbers to name every new object, so any build that creates new objects shifts what comes after.

An old build that only creates 36 extra empty meshes differed from the shipped build in the night view by 11,082 pixels: a faint shimmer on the dome halves, whose painted texture uses random numbers. The new build differs by exactly the same 11,082 pixels. In real play the random numbers are not seeded, so that texture already differs from one load to the next. The tool that loads two builds is kept as tools/pixel-compare.mjs, with these limits written in it. The same-frame check is the one that proves the picture.

  • as shipped: 8.96, 8.91, 8.62, 9.49, 9.13 and 9.41 ms;
  • new: 8.85, 8.52, 8.94, 9.14, 8.89 and 9.31 ms.

The test machine is a desktop processor. A phone’s processor spends longer on each draw call, so the same cut in calls should save more there in absolute terms. This was not measured.

The software renderer’s time per pass

The software renderer in the headless browser is a rough stand-in for a graphics chip. At these four spots, each pass took as long as before, within run-to-run variation. Two examples:

  • the landing view: 6,094 ms before, 6,096 ms after;
  • the landing shadow map: 303 ms before, 300 ms after.

The software renderer spends its time filling pixels, so the grass’s saved vertex work does not show there. Whether it shows on the phone’s graphics chip was not measured.

Tried and left out

  • Tiling the far mountains. Splitting the distant ring into 4, 6 or 8 tiles so each could be left out of view saved only 34–66 thousand triangles. It added 8–23 draw calls a frame, so it was reverted.
  • Ground blocks one level closer. Building the blocks from the second level of detail as well gave no fewer calls where it was measured.
  • Leaving far tree detail out of the near shadow cascade. It would change which triangles reach the near cascade at its edge, so it is not exact.
  • Leaving near casters out of the far cascade. It would save about 12 calls, and it is not exact where the cascades blend.
  • Merging the props for the view as well as for the shadow. The props on the road use 28 materials, many on one small mesh each. Merging them for the view means placing their normals ahead of time, which rounds differently. It also changes the order in which equally deep surfaces are drawn. Both would change pixels.
Limits

What this report cannot show

  • Nothing was tested on a real iPhone. A desktop processor and a software renderer stand in for the phone. How much faster the phone runs, if at all, is not known from here.
  • The pixel checks ran on the software renderer. The merged shadow casters use the same arithmetic as three.js’s own depth shader. A different shader compiler, such as the one on the iPhone’s graphics chip, could still fuse a multiply and an add differently in one and not the other, and shift a depth value in its last bit. That would move a shadow edge by at most a texel in rare places, and was not checked on the phone.
  • Ten views are not every view. They cover day, evening and night, the landing, the meadow, the coast road, the memorial, the observatory, the mountains and the hot rod. The changes are general rules, not tuned per view, but a view not drawn was not checked.
  • The sources below could not be read again from here. Outbound requests from this environment were blocked: curl got a refusal from the network proxy. The claims about three.js were checked in its source as installed with the game (version 0.186.1). The other references are cited from knowledge of them, not re-read today.
What would change this conclusion

What would change this conclusion

  • A trace on the iPhone showing the graphics chip, not the processor, as the limit. The timeline in Safari’s Web Inspector would show this. If so, draw calls matter less on the phone, and this round’s savings will hardly be felt. The work left would be in pixels: the 4× multisampled half-float view, the bloom chain, the 4096 by 2048 shadow atlas and the pixel ratio of 2. Every one of those changes the picture when lowered.
  • A trace showing the processor as the limit, with draw calls costing much more than here. Then this round saves more in absolute terms than measured, and the next exact step is the same per-part-matrix method applied to the view pass for the props with a material of their own. It is harder to make exact, because lit materials use the matrix in several places.
  • A shadow edge that looks different on the phone than on this build’s software check. That would point at the shader compiler difference described above. Switching the shadow casters off (shadowBatch.enabled = false) restores the old path without touching anything else.
  • New objects added to a site later with cut-out textures or their own depth material. They will simply cast on their own, as before. They lose nothing, but they gain nothing either.
